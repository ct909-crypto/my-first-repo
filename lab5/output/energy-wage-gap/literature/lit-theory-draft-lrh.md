## Background and theory

### Good jobs in the energy transition?

Decarbonizing electricity is often presented as an employment opportunity as well as an environmental necessity. In 2023 clean-energy employment grew more than twice as fast as the rest of the energy sector and the U.S. economy, and electric power generation is one of its main sites [@doe2024useer]. Whether this growth amounts to a just transition depends on more than job counts. The just-transition idea, developed within the labor movement and then taken up by energy-justice scholars, holds that the burdens and benefits of decarbonization should not fall on the workers and communities least able to absorb them [@ilo2014just; @newell2013political; @stevis2015global; @mccauley2018just; @healy2017politicizing]. Reviews of the field now call for attention to the distributive consequences of supply-side decarbonization for workers, not only for households and regions [@beckfield2023social; @carley2020justice; @sovacool2019decarbonization]. In the United States, fossil-fuel communities are highly exposed to the transition [@raimi2022mapping], and qualitative research finds that energy workers judge the transition largely by what it would do to their pay, skills, and bargaining power [@sicotte2022necessary; @mayer2018just; @cha2020just]. Job quality in this sense is a core component of good jobs [@kalleberg2011good]. The question is therefore not only whether renewable energy creates jobs but whether the jobs it creates pay like the ones they partly replace.

### What we know about green and renewable wages

Evidence on green wages is growing but inconsistent, partly because studies compare different things. Task-based measures suggest that green-intensive employment in U.S. local labor markets carried a modest wage premium of about four percent [@vona2019measures], and green jobs in Japan earn more than non-green jobs largely because of their task content [@kuai2025estimating]. Using online job postings, @curtis2023green find that solar and wind jobs are created in occupations that pay about 21 percent more than the average job. Their measure, however, assigns each posting the national average wage of its occupation. It describes the occupational composition of renewable hiring, not what renewable employers pay a given occupation. Studies that observe employers directly are less optimistic. Linked employer-employee data show that, conditional on education, workers do not earn more in clean firms than in legacy energy firms, and that moves from legacy to clean firms are rare [@colmer2025nice]. Fewer than one percent of workers leaving carbon-intensive jobs move into green ones [@curtis2024workers]. Norwegian evidence points to a green-firm premium that remains smaller than the premium paid in high-carbon industries [@godoy2025green]. Industry reports show that median wages differ across energy technologies, with solar at the low end [@lehmann2020wages]. Reviews conclude that green-job research relies on inconsistent definitions and reference groups and seldom asks how rewards are distributed within the energy sector [@bradley2025empirical; @pearse2022labour; @hanna2024job].

These studies leave a specific gap. Occupation-level measures cannot tell whether a solar-plant electrician earns what a gas-plant electrician earns, and firm-level studies group heterogeneous technologies into a single "clean" category. No study that we know of holds detailed occupation constant while comparing pay across generation technologies. Nor do we know whether any such gap narrowed as renewable generation employment roughly doubled in the early 2020s. That period saw large federal investment in clean electricity, which ecological-modernization arguments would expect to raise the returns to green work.

### Why the same job pays differently across sectors

A large literature shows that where one works matters for pay, independent of what one does. Industries pay persistently different wages to observably similar workers, even after accounting for labor quality, working conditions, and firm size, which suggests that workers in high-wage industries share in rents [@krueger1988efficiency]. Employer effects account for a substantial part of wage variation [@abowd1999high], and rising dispersion between workplaces has driven much of the growth in earnings inequality [@card2013workplace; @barth2016where; @song2019firming; @tomaskovicdevey2020rising]. Sociologists interpret these patterns relationally. Organizations allocate rewards through claims-making in which workers' bargaining position, the organization's resources, and its institutional history shape who gets what [@tomaskovicdevey2019relational; @wilmers2021consolidated]. Categorical distinctions become durable when they are built into organizational routines that outlast the conditions that produced them [@tilly1998durable]. Unions, licensing, and other closure devices raise pay by restricting access and institutionalizing wage norms [@weeden2002why; @western2011unions; @rosenfeld2014unions]. Much of the gender and racial wage gap operates the same way: through the jobs and establishments people are sorted into and the pay attached to them, rather than through unequal pay within the same job [@petersen1995separate; @cohen2003individuals; @huffman2004racial].

Applied to power generation, this literature suggests that technology works as an organizational context, not only as an engineering choice. As @winner1980artifacts argued, energy technologies come with distinct forms of organization. Fossil-fuel and nuclear plants are large, capital-intensive, long-lived facilities, historically operated by regulated utilities with internal labor markets, formal job ladders, and comparatively dense collective bargaining. Wind and solar generation is newer, more modular, and more often developed by independent power producers and project-financed firms, with small permanent operations workforces. Survey data for 2023 show union or project-labor coverage of 16 to 19 percent in coal, natural gas, and nuclear generation, compared with 11 to 14 percent in solar and wind generation and 13 percent in water power [@doe2024useer]. If pay-setting institutions are the channel through which sector affects wages, then workers classified in the same detailed occupation should earn less in renewable generation than in nonrenewable generation. We therefore expect that, within the same detailed occupation, renewable generation establishments pay lower mean wages than nonrenewable establishments (H1). Because renewable hiring is concentrated in occupations that pay well on average [@curtis2023green], occupational composition should not explain the renewable disadvantage. It may even mask part of it. We expect the within-occupation renewable gap to be at least as large as the raw gap in average pay (H5).

### Technologies, occupations, and time

The same argument implies heterogeneity. If the gap reflects the organizational form of newer technologies rather than a property of "renewable" energy as such, it should be concentrated in solar, wind, and the small biomass and geothermal segments. It should be negligible for hydroelectric generation, a mature renewable technology whose plants were largely built under the utility model. Nuclear generation, the most institutionally consolidated technology, should pay at least as much as fossil generation. We expect the within-occupation gap relative to fossil generation to be largest for solar and wind, near zero for hydroelectric generation, and non-negative for nuclear generation (H2). Institutional protections also matter unequally across occupations. Collective bargaining and internal labor markets have historically raised and compressed pay most for manual and craft workers, while managerial and professional pay is more often benchmarked against external markets that span employers [@western2011unions; @kalleberg2011good; @diprete2010compensation]. Union debates over renewable energy turn largely on the fear that the manual and craft jobs it creates will not carry the protections these workers hold in legacy plants [@sicotte2025labor; @vachon2023clean]. We therefore expect the renewable gap to be larger in blue-collar occupational groups (construction and extraction; installation, maintenance, and repair; production) than in professional and managerial groups (H3).

Finally, two perspectives make competing predictions about change over time. Ecological modernization treats environmental reform as a source of economic upgrading [@spaargaren1992sociology]. From that view, rapid growth, tight skilled-labor markets, and federal subsidies with prevailing-wage and apprenticeship provisions should pull renewable pay toward legacy levels. Critics of ecological modernization, and the durable-inequality perspective, stress that institutional arrangements adjust slowly and that growth need not transform pay structures [@york2003key; @tilly1998durable; @knuth2019whatever; @burke2018political]. Worker-level studies of the transition's political economy point the same way: the terms of renewable employment are set by coalitions and bargaining capacity, not by growth alone [@hess2012good; @sicotte2025labor]. Following the durable-inequality view, we expect the within-occupation renewable gap not to narrow between the earliest and latest periods observed (H4). The rival expectation of narrowing is the ecological-modernization prediction.
