## Background and theory

### Good jobs in the energy transition?

Decarbonizing electricity is often framed as an employment opportunity as well as an environmental necessity. In 2023, clean-energy employment in the United States grew more than twice as fast as the rest of the energy sector and the economy as a whole, and electric power generation is one of its main sites [@doe2024useer]. Whether this expansion amounts to a just transition depends on more than job counts. The just-transition idea originated in the labor movement as a demand that workers not bear the costs of environmental policy [@ilo2014just; @stevis2015global]. Energy-justice scholars later extended it into a broader principle for sharing the burdens and benefits of decarbonization [@newell2013political; @mccauley2018just; @healy2017politicizing]. A recent review of the sociology of supply-side decarbonization calls for closer attention to what the shift away from fossil fuels means for workers and their communities [@beckfield2023social].<!--ev: beckfield2023social-45b1338a--> Related work treats the distribution of costs and benefits as a central justice question for the clean-energy transition [@carley2020justice; @sovacool2019decarbonization]. In the United States, fossil-fuel communities are highly exposed to these changes [@raimi2022mapping]. Interviews with unionized energy workers show that their expectations of the transition depend on whether their skills fit renewable industries and whether conditions there would strengthen or weaken their bargaining power [@sicotte2022necessary].<!--ev: sicotte2022necessary-a6e0c50a--> Pay is a core component of job quality [@kalleberg2011good]. The question for a just transition is therefore not only whether renewable energy creates jobs but whether the jobs it creates pay like the ones they partly replace.

### What we know about green and renewable wages

Evidence on green wages is growing but inconsistent, partly because studies compare different quantities. A task-based measure applied to U.S. local labor markets in 2006-2014 finds that green employment carried a wage premium of about four percent [@vona2019measures].<!--ev: vona2019measures-1f401b7f--> In Japan, green jobs also pay more, a difference attributed mainly to their task content [@kuai2025estimating]. Using online job postings, @curtis2023green estimate that solar and wind openings cluster in occupations whose average pay is roughly a fifth above the national mean.<!--ev: curtis2023green-1fd7f723--> Their measure, however, gives each posting the economy-wide average wage of its occupation. It captures the occupational mix of renewable hiring, not what renewable employers pay for a given job. Studies that observe employers directly are less optimistic. Linked employer-employee records indicate that clean-energy firms pay no more than legacy energy firms once workers' education is held constant, and that few workers move from legacy to clean employers [@colmer2025nice].<!--ev: colmer2025nice-93aec07f--> Transitions from carbon-intensive into green jobs have risen quickly but still absorb under one percent of those leaving carbon-intensive work [@curtis2024workers].<!--ev: curtis2024workers-9034f59e--> Norwegian evidence points to a green-firm premium that remains below the premium paid in high-carbon industries [@godoy2025green]. Industry reports show that median wages differ across energy technologies, with solar near the bottom [@lehmann2020wages]. A recent review concludes that green-jobs research relies on inconsistent definitions and comparison groups [@bradley2025empirical]. Reviews of low-carbon job creation have concentrated on the number of jobs rather than their pay [@hanna2024job], and value-theoretical work argues that the renewable labor process itself deserves scrutiny [@pearse2022labour].

These studies leave a specific gap. Occupation-level measures cannot say whether an electrician at a solar plant earns what an electrician at a gas plant earns. Firm-level studies, for their part, group heterogeneous technologies into a single "clean" category. We know of no study that holds detailed occupation constant while comparing pay across generation technologies. Nor do we know whether such a gap narrowed as renewable generation employment expanded rapidly in the early 2020s [@doe2024useer]. That expansion coincided with large federal investment in clean electricity, which ecological-modernization arguments would expect to raise the returns to green work [@spaargaren1992sociology].

### Why the same job pays differently across sectors

A large literature shows that where people work matters for pay, independent of what they do. Industry wage differentials persist when worker skill, job conditions, and employer size are held constant, a pattern that @krueger1988efficiency interpret as evidence that high-wage industries pay above market-clearing rates.<!--ev: krueger1988efficiency-24268d09--> Employer effects account for a substantial share of wage variation [@abowd1999high]. Growing pay differences between workplaces account for a large share of the rise in earnings inequality, in Germany [@card2013workplace] and in the United States [@barth2016where; @song2019firming], and between-workplace inequality has risen across high-income countries [@tomaskovicdevey2020rising].<!--ev: card2013workplace-9192c57b--> Sociologists interpret these patterns relationally. Organizations distribute rewards through claims-making in which workers' bargaining position, the organization's resources, and its institutional history shape who gets what [@tomaskovicdevey2019relational; @wilmers2021consolidated]. Categorical distinctions become durable when they are built into the routines of organizations and outlast the conditions that produced them [@tilly1998durable]. Social closure through licensing and unionization raises pay for those inside the boundary [@weeden2002why], and unions have historically compressed wages and upheld pay norms beyond their own members [@western2011unions; @rosenfeld2014unions].<!--ev: western2011unions-27a5653b--> Sorting follows the same logic. Much of the gender wage gap reflects the occupations and establishments that women and men are sorted into rather than unequal pay within the same job [@petersen1995separate], and the wages attached to jobs depend on who typically holds them [@cohen2003individuals; @huffman2004racial].

Applied to power generation, this literature suggests treating technology as an organizational context as well as an engineering choice. @winner1980artifacts argued, with nuclear and solar power as examples, that technologies can be bound up with particular arrangements of authority and organization. We conjecture that a similar logic applies to employment relations in generation. Fossil-fuel and nuclear plants are large, capital-intensive, long-lived facilities, many of them built under the regulated-utility model with internal labor markets and formal job ladders. Wind and solar generation is newer and more modular, and its permanent operations workforces are small. Union and project-labor coverage in 2023 was 16 to 19 percent in coal, natural gas, and nuclear generation, compared with 11 to 14 percent in solar and wind generation and 13 percent in water power [@doe2024useer].<!--ev: doe2024useer-7867bd4b, doe2024useer-d6a78790--> If pay-setting institutions link sector to wages, workers in the same detailed occupation should earn less in renewable generation than in nonrenewable generation. We therefore expect that, within the same detailed occupation, renewable generation establishments pay lower mean wages than nonrenewable establishments (H1). Because renewable hiring is concentrated in occupations that pay well on average [@curtis2023green], occupational composition should not account for the renewable disadvantage and may even conceal part of it. We therefore expect the within-occupation renewable gap to be at least as large as the raw gap in average pay (H5).<!--ev: curtis2023green-1fd7f723-->

### Technologies, occupations, and time

The same argument implies heterogeneity. If the gap reflects the organizational form of newer technologies rather than a property of renewable energy as such, it should be concentrated in solar and wind generation and in the small biomass and geothermal segments. It should be negligible for hydroelectric generation, a mature renewable technology whose plants long predate the recent build-out. Nuclear generation, the most institutionally consolidated technology, should pay at least as much as fossil generation. We expect the within-occupation gap relative to fossil generation to be largest for solar and wind, near zero for hydroelectric generation, and non-negative for nuclear generation (H2). Institutional protections also matter unequally across occupations. Collective bargaining has historically raised and compressed pay most for manual and craft workers [@western2011unions; @kalleberg2011good]. At the top of organizations, pay is increasingly benchmarked against external peer groups rather than internal scales [@diprete2010compensation]. Union strategies toward renewable energy are shaped by the concern that the manual and craft jobs it creates will not carry the protections these workers hold in legacy plants [@sicotte2025labor; @vachon2023clean]. We therefore expect the renewable gap to be larger in blue-collar occupational groups (construction and extraction; installation, maintenance, and repair; production) than in professional and managerial groups (H3).

Finally, two perspectives make competing predictions about change over time. Ecological modernization treats environmental reform as a source of economic upgrading [@spaargaren1992sociology]. From that view, rapid growth and federal subsidies tied to prevailing-wage and apprenticeship standards should pull renewable pay toward legacy levels. Critics question whether modernization delivers the institutional change its proponents expect [@york2003key], and research on the politics of renewable energy emphasizes that its terms are set by contested coalitions and uneven power rather than by growth alone [@burke2018political; @knuth2019whatever; @hess2012good].<!--ev: york2003key-927ecfc9--> A durable-inequality reading adds that organizational pay structures adjust slowly [@tilly1998durable]. Following this view, we expect the within-occupation renewable gap not to narrow between the earliest and latest periods observed (H4), while the ecological-modernization view predicts narrowing. Official occupational wage estimates pool three years of survey panels and are not designed as a time series [@bls2025oews; @bls2025oewsfaq].<!--ev: bls2025oews-4b53ed44, bls2025oewsfaq-2b53f70b--> We therefore test H4 by comparing estimates built from non-overlapping panels, as described in the next section.
